PART 1 • MULTI-PROBE STEERING

5. Multi-probe steering

At 16 probes, readout error reduction is 95.9% for direction, 95.0% for speed, and 94.5% for acceleration.

Multi-probe steering (75 s). Minimum-change least-squares edits in the A-probe subspace preserve its orthogonal complement. Direction targets every 45 degrees; scalars use low/mid/high targets. Readout error reduction is 100 × (1 − mean edited absolute target error / mean unedited absolute target error), using the same D clips and requested targets in numerator and denominator. This is a ratio of aggregate errors, not a mean of per-example percentages. Zero means no improvement; 100% means the target is reached exactly; negative values mean worse than no edit. It is not R² and does not measure downstream velocity response. At 16 probes the reductions are 95.85%, 95.01%, and 94.50%. Sixteen is a fixed illustrative setting, not a D-selected optimum. Full sequences increase edit sizes and can reduce readout error reduction. These are pooled independent-readout results; later encoder blocks and predictor are not run. Construction success follows from satisfying the probe constraints; transfer to the independent evaluator is the test.

INTERPRETATION

6. Readout agreement does not establish causality

The correlation persists across independent readouts; a causal role in predicted motion remains untested by this experiment.

Interpretation (45 s). Multi-probe steering confirms that target-directed edits transfer from the construction probes to an independent C-trained evaluator on D clips. This is stronger than satisfying the construction probes, but both can depend on the same correlates. The intervention causes a change in the external evaluator output; it does not by itself establish that the physical variable controls the model’s predicted motion. This pooled experiment does not run the remaining encoder blocks or predictor. The later prefix-token experiment addresses that missing downstream test by editing velocity and measuring predicted position through a frozen readout. That test still requires attention to readout validity and off-target effects; failure of the pooled test to establish causality is not proof of noncausality.

PART 2 • SPLINE CONSTRUCTION

7. Centroid splines

A-only PCA64 + binned centroids; B selects 8–16 knots.

Spline construction (80 s). Fits are label-conditioned centroids, not a learned full activation manifold. A fits the 64D PCA and centroid curves; B selects 12 direction knots, 8 speed knots and 12 acceleration knots by reconstruction error at known labels. PCA retains about 98% of activation variance. Direction periodicity is imposed; direction data combine two motion regimes. Every spline result presented uses an offset-preserving centroid-difference edit with the known source label. The same offset from the source centroid is retained at the target, and the PCA orthogonal complement is unchanged. This does not guarantee membership in the full activation manifold. The appendix illustrates this edit. Camera views: direction elevation 22°, azimuth −55°; speed and acceleration elevation 20°, azimuth 55°.

8. Spline versus linear steering

Linear steering gives greater readout error reduction for all three variables; both methods improve substantially over no edit.

Pooled readout comparison (60 s). Readout error reduction is 100 × (1 − mean edited absolute target error / mean unedited absolute target error), using the same D clips and requested targets in numerator and denominator. This is a ratio of aggregate errors, not a mean of per-example percentages. Zero means no improvement; 100% means the target is reached exactly; negative values mean worse than no edit. EXTENSION • ITERATED TOKEN STEERING

10. Iterated velocity steering

Matched one-probe gain 3.45% → all eligible probes 17.91%.

Iterated token steering (90 s). Each token gets its own sequence; original position-probe directions are protected. Steering eligibility requires both velocity components to have B R² ≥ 0.5. Token fitting stops after three near-baseline probes or a 256-dimensional cap. There are 4,274 eligible probes across 1,024 tokens; the median is 3 and maximum 23. The cap-32 condition therefore saturates the eligible set. Random controls match per-token edit norm and preserve construction position readouts. Independent context-position drift grows to 0.367 px. The initial historical joint four-output fit gave 3.33%; the matched one-probe condition shown here gives 3.45%. This supports an intervention response in the measured pipeline, not “18% of all causal information.” Full erasure has substantial position confounding; see the appendix.

EXTENSION • SPEED STEERING

11. Predicted motion: spline versus linear

Spline steering achieves 5.39% of the requested velocity change; iterated linear steering achieves 17.91%.

Main predictor comparison (60 s). The spline edit adds f(s + Δs) − f(s), using known source speed and broadcasting the same channel displacement to every token. It achieves 5.39% of the requested velocity change. Iterated linear steering achieves 17.91%, with separate token-specific directions and position-readout constraints. Thus this compares the two implemented methods; it does not isolate the effect of using a linear versus nonlinear function class. Gain is the measured change in velocity, inferred from predicted positions, divided by the requested change. The plotted intervals are 95% clip-bootstrap intervals conditional on the fitted models. Unsuccessful spline variants have been omitted from the presentation; their full results remain in the experiment reports.

CONCLUSIONS

12. Conclusions

We have evidence of causal sensitivity, not complete or physically validated counterfactual control.

Conclusion (60 s). Accurate readout and redundant representations are established on these clips. All spline results presented use the centroid-difference edit that preserves the source offset. The fixed linear setting achieves greater pooled readout error reduction, but that metric alone does not validate downstream steering or preservation of unrelated scene properties. In the speed-only predictor test, iterated linear edits give 17.91% response versus 5.39% for spline edits. These methods differ in token mapping and position constraints. Avoid claiming that splines universally fail, that the offset-preserving method guarantees manifold membership, or that 18% of causal information has been captured. The predictor response is measured through a frozen position readout, with limited counterfactual validation.

APPENDIX A • METHODS

13. Spline steering preserves the offset

Carry the original offset to the target centroid; requesting no change gives exactly zero edit.

The diagram illustrates the single spline edit used throughout this presentation. In PCA coordinates z, add f(target) − f(source): z_new = z + f(target) − f(source). The resulting offset z_new − f(target) is exactly z − f(source). Lift this difference through the PCA basis to edit the activation; the orthogonal component is unchanged. The method uses the known source label, and requesting that same label gives exactly zero edit. In the predictor experiment, the lifted channel displacement is added to every token. This preserves the centroid-curve offset, not necessarily all unrelated information or membership in the full activation manifold. The diagram is illustrative rather than a measured PCA projection.

APPENDIX • EVALUATION LIMITS

14. Position-readout limitations

An intervention effect is established in the measured pipeline; natural counterfactual validity remains open.

The three baselines use all 256 D clips. The frozen C-trained position readout applied to unedited predictions has a farthest-frame error of 8.17 px. This beats holding the last observed position (21.26 px), but measured-prefix trajectory extrapolation is much better (0.78 px). Evaluators were not trained on edited examples, so out-of-distribution readout behavior remains possible. Bootstrap intervals condition on fitted models and omit training-seed uncertainty. No edited video was generated.
